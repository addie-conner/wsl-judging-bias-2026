# Surfing Is the Test Case

## Every other subjective sport got its judging reform after a scandal. Surfing has the data and not the scandal — which makes it the experiment for whether evidence alone is enough to fix a sport

_A note before we start: this piece is about how subjective-sport governance has historically responded to evidence of bias. Specific judges and athletes are not the subject. Subjective-sport judging systems produce measurable, identity-correlated patterns unless transparency and panel design actively counteract them. That is the universal claim of the cross-sport literature. The question is what gets a sport to act on it._

---

## I. Marie-Reine Le Gougne

The first thing she said was that she had been pressured.

It was the night of February 11, 2002, the morning after the Olympic pairs figure-skating final at Salt Lake City. Marie-Reine Le Gougne was a French international judge, fifty-two years old, twenty years on the ISU judging roster. She had just placed Russian pair Berezhnaya and Sikharulidze first ahead of the Canadian pair Salé and Pelletier in a result the live audience had booed. Three hours after the final, in a closed meeting with referee Sally Stapleford and the rest of the technical committee, Le Gougne broke down and admitted that the head of the French federation, Didier Gailhaguet, had instructed her to vote for the Russians in exchange for Russian votes for the French ice-dance team in the same Olympics.

The confession was the moment subjective-sport judging changed.

Within seventy-two hours, the IOC awarded a duplicate gold to the Canadians. Within four days, the ISU suspended Le Gougne and Gailhaguet. Within ten weeks, the ISU announced the architectural overhaul of figure-skating scoring that would eventually become the International Judging System — anonymous panels, randomized score selection, larger judging cohorts, post-event score release. The 6.0 system that had governed figure skating for ninety years was retired.

The reform took eighteen months to design and seven years to fully implement. The data on whether it worked is still being argued about today. Twenty-four years later, the 2026 Milan-Cortina Games still produced a score-pattern in which a documented majority of home-country judges scored their compatriots above the panel average, and *Sportico* wrote it up as breaking news.

But the reform happened. Reform happened because Marie-Reine Le Gougne broke down in a hallway and confessed.

This is the story of what happens to subjective sports when they get scandals. It is also the story of what hasn't happened to surfing — yet — and what that absence means.

---

## II. The cascade

Salt Lake City was the start of a sixteen-year cascade.

In the summer of 2004 in Athens, the men's gymnastics all-around final produced a different version of the same problem. The Korean gymnast Yang Tae-young had executed a parallel-bars routine assigned a start value of 9.9 by the FIG judging panel. The American Paul Hamm scored 9.837 on his vault dismount and won gold by 0.012 points. Korea appealed the start-value calculation. The Court of Arbitration for Sport ruled that FIG had indeed miscalculated — by a factor that, properly applied, would have given Yang gold — but that the appeal had not been filed within the required window. Hamm kept the medal. The gymnastics world spent two years arguing.

In 2006, FIG abandoned the 10.0 holistic scale and introduced the D-score / E-score Code of Points: a constructed score from sub-categories, with the difficulty rating computed mechanically and the execution rating handed out by panel. The reform was direct response to Athens. The reform did not eliminate national bias — Heiniger and Mercier later showed bias persists at smaller magnitude in the apparatus events where execution scoring is most holistic — but it changed the architecture of the score in a way that made bias structurally harder.

Then boxing.

The Rio Olympics of 2016 produced a sequence of disputed decisions so consistent that the AIBA — boxing's governing body — suspended its own judges from active duty mid-tournament. Vasiliy Lomachenko, Robson Conceição, the entire 56kg light-flyweight bracket: each produced a controversy that the data and the broadcast both confirmed. AIBA spent the next six years trying to reform itself and failing. In 2022, the McLaren independent report concluded that AIBA's judging governance was structurally compromised. The IOC suspended AIBA's recognition, and in 2023 made the call: boxing was excluded from the Paris 2024 Games.

The IOC has shown it is willing to use the nuclear option when judging governance becomes structurally indefensible. *Boxing is the precedent.*

Reform came to figure skating because of a confession in a hallway. Reform came to gymnastics because of a CAS ruling and a scoring miscalculation that proved the architecture was broken. Reform came to boxing — eventually, after years of denial — because the IOC removed the sport from the world's biggest stage.

What did all three sports have in common? A scandal. A specific moment. A confession, a court ruling, an exclusion. Something the governing body could not explain away. Reform did not come from cumulative academic literature. It did not come from years of journal articles documenting bias at the 0.04 to 0.32 point range across various predictors. It came from a public, undeniable, narratively coherent rupture.

## III. The reforms that worked, and what they tell us

The cross-sport reform record is, by now, fairly clear.

The single most-effective single intervention is **public per-judge data with reputation-cost discipline**. Eric Zitzewitz documented this in his 2014 paper on figure skating. After the IJS introduced delayed publication of individual judge scores with names attached, the categories of identifiable bias measured in the academic literature dropped substantially — in some categories by what amounted to roughly half, in others by smaller amounts. The exact number depends on which bias and which subset of the data, but the direction was always the same: reputational visibility suppresses repeated-pattern identifiable bias, because individual judges become accountable for behavior that was previously lost in the trim-mean.

The mechanism is informational rather than coercive. A judge whose individual scores are public will face career consequences for repeated, visible, patterned bias. The threat of those consequences is enough to move scoring behavior. It does not eliminate bias; it disciplines it. Twenty-four years after the IJS reform, Akabas's 2026 *Sportico* analysis still shows compatriot bias in figure skating — but at smaller magnitude than the 6.0 era and with the judges' own scores publicly attributable.

The second most-effective intervention is **scoring-scale architecture that prevents the bias channel at the input layer**. Olympic diving has used a 0.5-increment 0–10 scale since the 1970s. Round-number anchoring at the .25 or .75 level cannot occur on input — those values do not exist on the diving scale. Gymnastics' 2006 D-score / E-score split has the same property at a different layer: it forces judges to construct a final score from sub-categories rather than generate it holistically, which makes anchoring-on-the-final-number cognitively much harder. *The architecture of the scoring system can substitute, partially, for transparency reforms.*

The third intervention is **panel-rotation rules that prevent compatriot stacking**. Gymnastics implemented a version of this post-Athens. Figure skating uses randomized panel selection within IJS. The mechanism is mechanical: if no judge from country X is allowed on the panel scoring an athlete from country X, the compatriot panel-composition channel is structurally eliminated. The behavior of any individual judge is irrelevant; the architecture of the panel does the work.

Each reform addresses a different bias channel. Per-judge transparency disciplines individual behavior. Scoring-scale architecture eliminates the input pathway. Panel rotation eliminates the composition pathway. They are partially independent. They compound.

The cross-sport literature, taken together, supports a rough magnitude estimate: a sport that implements all three reforms tends to see substantial reduction in identifiable-bias channels in the seasons that follow — typically on the order of a third to a half on the categories that have been measured. The original Zitzewitz (2014) figure-skating result documented this for compatriot bias post-IJS at roughly 40-50 percent for the cleanest categories; Heiniger and Mercier's gymnastics work (2018, 2021) found apparatus-dependent reductions in the same general range post-2006 Code of Points. Not elimination. Substantial reduction. The remaining bias is the residual that no public reform can fully address — the shared subjective prior that emerges from common training pipelines, common broadcast exposures, common cultural framings of what a "good" performance looks like.

## IV. The exception

Surfing's complaints about judging are not new. Brazilian-judge ratios, Australian home-event boosts, round-number clustering, day-of-event amplification — these have been comments-section topics for fifteen years. The fan-side narrative is loud, repetitive, and not entirely wrong: the bias mechanisms exist, in measurable form, in the data we have now assembled.

What surfing has not had is a Marie-Reine Le Gougne. There has been no confession. No CAS ruling. No IOC exclusion. The judging body has never been confronted with a discrete public moment of the kind that has historically forced subjective sports to reform.

The closest surfing has come is the World Surf League's quiet panel-composition shift after October 2023, when Renato Pereira replaced Pritamo Ahrendt as head judge. Brazilian-judge counts on Brazilian-surfer panels fell by half over three years. The shift was not announced as a reform. There was no press release. The data shows the change; the WSL has never publicly described it.

That is not the same as a Le Gougne moment.

It is, however, an interesting indicator. It suggests the WSL judging body is internally responsive to identified patterns even without public pressure. It also suggests that without public pressure, the response will not be announced, will not be branded, will not be evaluated, and will not be made durable. Reform that happens quietly can also be reversed quietly.

## V. The experiment

This is where surfing becomes scientifically interesting in a way no other subjective sport has been.

Every other reform in the historical record came after a scandal. The implicit hypothesis in the cross-sport literature — although it is rarely stated this directly — has been that subjective-sport governing bodies require a scandal to reform. Data alone has never been sufficient. Academic publication has never been sufficient. Even the most rigorously documented bias evidence, accumulating over decades in figure skating and gymnastics, did not move the governing bodies until something happened that those bodies could not deny.

Surfing is now the natural experiment for whether that hypothesis is correct.

The data on surf-judging bias is now assembled — 60,834 panel-trim-mean wave-scores, 301,478 individual judge-scoring decisions, seventeen seasons, the largest corpus available for any subjective sport. The bias mechanisms are documented. The cross-sport reform menu is well-understood. The implementation costs are operational rather than structural. There is no methodological barrier. There is no financial barrier. There is, at the moment, no scandal forcing the question.

What happens between now and the 2028 Olympic surfing event in Los Angeles will tell us whether *evidence alone* is sufficient to drive subjective-sport reform.

If the WSL and the ISA implement the cross-sport-tested reform menu — per-judge transparency, scoring-scale architecture, panel-rotation rules — before LA28, surfing will be the first subjective sport in the historical record to reform without a Le Gougne. The cross-sport hypothesis fails. Evidence is sufficient.

If they don't, the LA28 surfing event will produce, under partisan crowds and host-country panel composition and the same cognitive anchoring channels we have measured, the bias signatures the data forecasts. The cross-sport hypothesis holds. Evidence is not sufficient. Subjective sports require scandals.

Realistically, the most likely outcome is partial: ISA implements one or two reforms but not the full menu before LA28, or pilots reforms that do not fully take effect in time for the Olympic competition. Under partial implementation, the SHA-locked predictions resolve mixed — some forecasts falsified in the predicted direction, others resolving close to the unreformed baseline. That outcome is informative too. It tells us which reforms are tractable for which governance structures, and on what timeline. It is the empirical-rather-than-rhetorical answer to a question the cross-sport literature has had no clean way to address.

Either way — full reform, no reform, partial reform — the data will resolve the predictions. The reform will happen, or it won't, or it will partially happen, and the cross-sport hypothesis becomes empirically rather than historically tractable.

---

## VI. What's at stake

There is a version of the surfing story that ends the way figure skating ended in 2003 and gymnastics ended in 2006: with a public reform, a structural overhaul, and a cleaner sport for the next twenty years. That version requires the WSL and the ISA to act on evidence rather than on scandal, which is a thing no subjective-sport governing body has done in the recorded history of subjective-sport governance.

There is another version of the surfing story that ends the way boxing's story ended: with a sport that ignored the cumulative evidence, defended its judging legitimacy with public-relations talking points, and was eventually told by the IOC that it would not be in the next Games until governance changed. The IOC has used that move once. There is no architectural reason it could not use it again.

Surfing is, at this moment, somewhere between those two versions. The data is published or about to be. The reform menu is on the table. The panel-rotation move that the WSL has already, quietly, made is evidence of internal capacity to respond. The question is whether the ISA will follow, whether the reforms will be announced, whether they will be made structural rather than informal, and whether they will be implemented in time for LA28 to be judged under a reformed regime rather than the unreformed one.

Marie-Reine Le Gougne broke down in a hallway twenty-four years ago and the figure-skating world rebuilt its judging system because of it. Yang Tae-young's parallel-bars start value brought down the gymnastics 10.0 scale a couple of years later. Boxing waited a decade longer and got removed from an Olympics for its trouble.

Surfing has access to all that history. It has the evidence. It has the cross-sport reform menu, already tested.

What it doesn't have is a scandal.

What it has instead is a paper, a pre-registered set of forecasts, and an Olympic event in twenty-four months on American home soil at one of the most-photographed surf venues in the world.

We're about to find out whether that's enough.
