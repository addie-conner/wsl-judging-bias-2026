Dear Editor,

I am submitting *Manufacturing Consensus: Mechanisms of Subjective Bias in Professional Surf Judging* for consideration as an empirical research article in the *Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports*.

The manuscript reports findings on the largest publicly-available corpus of professional surf-judging data assembled to date — 60,834 panel-trim-mean wave scores and 301,478 individual judge-scoring decisions from the World Surf League Championship Tour, 2009–2026, with judge-nationality coverage on 86.3% of judge-score values. The corpus is approximately 15× the size of Santos et al. (2025) and 3× the size of the IJSF (2025) WSL judging study at the panel-trim-mean level, with a per-judge layer that does not exist in the prior published WSL literature.

The paper makes four contributions. First, it documents a within-WSL institutional-accountability finding (the H32 result): mean Brazilian-judge count on Brazilian-surfer panels declined from 1.72 in 2018 to 0.84 in 2026, with a permutation test under within-event year-shuffles returning p < 0.001 over 1,000 reps, a Callaway–Sant'Anna staggered-DiD ATT of −0.90 panel-judges by 2026 against an 8-country donor pool, and a behavioural per-judge cross-validation. To my knowledge, no prior subjective-sport judging study has documented a within-governing-body institutional response to identified bias patterns mid-data — a finding with direct implications for the cross-sport reform-trigger literature.

Second, it replicates the Findlay & Ste-Marie (2004) reputation prior in surfing at heat-FE β = −0.013/rank (p = 1.8 × 10⁻⁴, n = 19,155); the Santos (2025) inter-judge agreement finding (within-surfer CV = 0.13); and round-number anchoring at 59.9% of individual judge-scores against a uniform null of 20% (3.0× ratio, replicating in a sealed 2025 women's CT hold-out at 25.2% trim-mean clustering versus 22.7% in training).

Third, the paper applies an 18-gate validation harness across the test family, including doubly-robust ML estimators (TMLE, Causal Forest, LinearDRLearner), Bayesian hierarchical modelling with posterior-predictive checks, sensitivity bounds (E-value, Rosenbaum Γ), and per-judge difference-in-differences identification. The Australian home-bloc estimate, naively +0.616 (p = 2 × 10⁻⁹), collapses under doubly-robust identification to point estimates of +0.005 (TMLE), −0.024 (Causal Forest), and +0.108 (per-judge DiD) with confidence intervals crossing zero — a methodologically informative result for the cross-sport compatriot-bias literature that has predominantly relied on cluster-robust OLS.

Fourth, the paper documents that the pre-registered unified-mechanism framework fails empirically on this corpus: cross-correlation between four primary mechanism vectors at the per-heat level returns mean |r| = 0.042, PCA PC1 explains 25.7% of variance, and Granger causality between mechanism dynamics is null. Bias mechanisms in WSL judging operate as statistically independent channels, with implications for theoretical models in the field that posit one underlying parameter.

The work was pre-registered at git SHA `7d0e2c8` on 2026-05-03 UTC, with hypotheses, specifications, and falsifiable effect-size predictions sealed before the analyses reported here. The 2025 women's Championship Tour (n = 1,815 panel-trim-mean wave-rows; sha256 `0c1616d755c3199430a094201209f282759da1fc3826cece0dfdce8f627c371a`) was held out at the same commit and opened only after training-period analyses were locked. All data, scripts, and analysis outputs are publicly available at the project replication archive (URL in the Data Availability Statement).

Two companion manuscripts extend the empirical findings reported here: a methodology paper detailing the 18-gate validation harness and per-judge difference-in-differences identification template (intended for *Annals of Applied Statistics* or *Journal of Educational and Behavioral Statistics*), and a policy paper proposing four reforms for the 2028 Los Angeles Olympic surfing event with a SHA-locked prospective forecast (intended for *Journal of Sports Economics* or similar). I am happy to share the companion manuscripts as preprints if they would be useful in evaluating this submission.

This manuscript is not under consideration at any other journal and has not been previously published, with the exception of a public-facing six-piece companion essay series for general readers, which will be released on Substack concurrently with the SportRxiv preprint of this manuscript. The Substack series cites the academic version as the source for all quantitative claims and does not contain new findings beyond those reported in this manuscript.
